# Rulebook

**Version:** 1.0.0 · **Last upgraded:** 2026-11-18 · **Videos learned from:** 0

This file holds two kinds of rules.

- **Core rules (`C-`)** come from Jake Trinder's playbook, which he says was built across 100+ channels. They're the starting point. They are never deleted, and only a PROVEN learned rule can mark one as "doesn't hold here".
- **Learned rules (`L-`)** are written by the self-upgrade protocol from *your* videos. They climb the evidence ladder described in `MASTER_PROMPT.md`.

Every rule ends with a **Check**, which is the question to ask at the gate.

**Versioning.** The PATCH number goes up when new evidence is added without changing any rule's level. MINOR goes up when a learned rule is added, promoted or retired. MAJOR goes up when a core rule is overridden or an engine default changes.

## Strategy

**C-01 · Viewer satisfaction is the target.** Optimize for what this viewer clicked for and exceed that exact expectation. CTR and retention are noisy diagnostics. The loop is that the right people click, they're satisfied, and YouTube tests the video on more people like them.
*Check: what did they click for, and how specifically do we beat it?*

**C-02 · One channel, one campaign, one core viewer.** Every video must appeal to the same underlying viewer. When each video attracts a different audience, satisfaction drops across the whole channel, and that drags every video down as the channel grows.
*Check: would the viewer this video attracts want to watch our last five videos?*

**C-03 · Your most-viewed video can be your least valuable.** An off-topic hit brings in people who ignore everything you publish next. Judge every video on the full funnel, not on views.
*Check: who did it bring in, and did they become leads?*

**C-04 · YouTube is winner-take-all.** For a given viewer, the video that wins the click and the watch takes most of the attention, both on the home feed and in search.
*Check: is ours better packaged and more satisfying than the best video already out there on this topic?*

## Demand (Stages 1–2)

**C-10 · No video without demand evidence.** Knowing your audience doesn't prove they'll choose the subject on YouTube.
*Check: what evidence shows our buyers already choose to watch this subject on YouTube?*

**C-11 · Outliers are relative.** A standout is a video that beat its own channel's normal views. Big raw numbers alone don't count. The engine measures this as views ÷ the median views of the neighbouring uploads.
*Check: how many times its channel's baseline did it get?*

**C-12 · Map the whole niche, not one competitor.** Use depth-first search: niche → search terms → outlier videos → their channels → standout videos → recommendations → back up a level → next branch. When a branch stops producing new evidence, take the next one.
*Check: have we explored every seed term in the market's language?*

**C-13 · Use the market's language.** Seed terms and titles should use the words buyers use to describe the problem ("lose visceral fat"), not our internal jargon.
*Check: would a buyer type these exact words?*

**C-14 · One viral video is a clue, not proof.** Look for repeated evidence before building around a topic or format. The engine's threshold is under *Engine defaults*.
*Check: how many independent outliers support this?*

**C-15 · Know the awareness level** (from *Breakthrough Advertising*). Viewers can want an outcome without understanding the mechanism behind it. If they want the outcome (fat loss) but not our mechanism (insulin), present the mechanism through the outcome. A boring or unknown mechanism attached to an outcome people want becomes what sets us apart.
*Check: does the outcome lead, and is the mechanism the new reason to care?*

**C-16 · If demand exists, build on it. If it doesn't, change the subject or connect it** to something people already want.
*Check: which proven desire does this connect to?*

**C-17 · Hunt for gaps.** A format that has worked in nearby niches but hasn't been used in ours yet is an opportunity. For example: new rules of SEO → new rules of LinkedIn → new rules of social media.
*Check: has this format worked elsewhere without being done here?*

**C-18 · Watch for signals outside YouTube.** A narrative that's catching on in short-form or in the wider market (e.g. "social media → interest media") can be the subject of the next long-form video.
*Check: what is our audience reacting to right now, on any platform?*

## Packaging (Stage 3)

**C-20 · The title and thumbnail make one promise together.** The viewer takes in the thumbnail first. The title then pins down the exact problem and promise. The two should add to each other, not repeat each other.
*Check: say the promise in one sentence. Do both elements serve it?*

**C-21 · A format is a proven way of framing a promise. Copy its reason, not its words.** Work out why people clicked: the desire, the psychological lever and what the viewer wanted from it. Then confirm your audience wants the same thing. "$1 vs. $10,000" works for entertainment viewers and makes no sense to a business audience.
*Check: why did people click the original, and is that true of our viewer?*

**C-22 · Judge formats on base rates, not on winners.** For any format, find out how many videos used it, what share became outliers, and why the losers failed. The cause is often execution: "I made 300 videos and learned this" works with 20–40 points and fails as a 3–5 point listicle.
*Check: what's the hit rate, and what did the failures do differently?*

**C-23 · The thumbnail must work in an instant.** In one glance, the right viewer should recognize the problem or outcome and see that the video is for them. Use things that viewer reads instantly:
- a visible transformation
- an authority cue, like a doctor in scrubs
- a subject that matches the audience, like a male body for a mostly male audience
- strong contrast, like white text on a red block over a dark background

*Check: shown for one second, does our viewer know what it is and that it's for them?*

**C-24 · Go as broad as you can without losing the core viewer.** Being specific costs reach. Pay that price only when it makes the right person feel more understood. The best titles pull in all of the core market and some of the broader market too ("If you don't understand insulin, you don't understand fat loss").
*Check: does broadening this make it less obvious to our buyer that it's for them?*

**C-25 · Test the packaging against the real feed.** Mock up the thumbnail and title next to the videos it will actually compete with. Then ask honest people from the target market which one they'd click.
*Check: does ours win the side-by-side?*

**C-26 · Formats can be invented.** Keep the psychology of a proven family of formats and build a new frame on it so you're first to market. "Shift" formats like "the new rules of…" and "…is about to change forever" are one such family. You can also pair a proven title format with a proven thumbnail format.
*Check: what proven psychology is this new frame built on?*

## Hook and delivery (Stages 4–5)

**C-30 · Open with Promise, Proof and Plan.** The Promise restates exactly what they clicked for. The Proof is the best reason to trust you on this topic. The Plan shows where the video goes, so they commit to the whole thing.
*Check: once the opening is over, do they know they picked the right video?*

**C-31 · Nail one authority statement and reuse it.** It's your unfair edge, said in one line and used across videos.
*Check: is the proof specific, verifiable and relevant to this topic?*

**C-32 · Long videos need a complete plan.** For courses and podcasts, make it unmistakable that they'll get everything ("the only X video you'll ever need… I'll cover A, B, C and D"). Podcasts need spoiler intros.
*Check: would a viewer feel safe committing to the full length?*

**C-33 · Deliver the format the packaging implied.** If the packaging promises a raw "show everything" video, deliver a raw, complete video, not a polished 15-minute summary.
*Check: does the production style match what the thumbnail and title led them to expect?*

**C-34 · Copy the execution, not just the title.** A result has more than one cause. Look at the structure (how many points), the delivery (whiteboard, raw footage, screen recording) and the pacing, and work out which of them made the original work.
*Check: how was the outlier executed, and are we matching it?*

**C-35 · Decide on scripting by video type.** Don't script screen recordings or challenge videos. Script talking-head videos that need tight copy and entertainment (open loops, smooth transitions). For everything else, give a sharp structure that leaves room to think, because a full script can make a strong expert worse on camera.
*Check: does exact wording matter here, or does natural delivery?*

**C-36 · Every visual must clarify, prove or help the viewer follow.** Anything else is decoration. Editing removes friction, but it can't rescue a weak idea or an empty video.
*Check: what does this visual do for the viewer's understanding?*

## Conversion (Stage 6)

**C-40 · The CTA is the viewer's most useful next step.** Avoid the usual pattern of value, value, value, then a random pitch.
*Check: at this moment in the video, what does the viewer need next?*

**C-41 · Apply the unbiased-expert test.** Would an unbiased expert include this product or offer because it makes the video more useful? If not, don't force it.
*Check: yes or no, and why?*

**C-42 · Put the product inside the story, where it removes a real constraint.** In the "sell a service in 10 hours" challenge, cold email was the only fast channel, and pre-warmed domains removed the two-week warm-up. Never make a "feature video".
*Check: does the product solve a problem the video itself ran into?*

**C-43 · If the offer can't be the tool, extend the lesson.** Offer an asset that helps the viewer act on the lesson, such as an ad library or a scorecard template, in exchange for their email.
*Check: after the lesson, what would a serious viewer want to have in hand?*

**C-44 · The video doesn't make the sale. It makes the next step obvious.** The opt-in moves viewers into the funnel, and email does the rest.
*Check: is there one clear next step, tracked to this video?*

## Data and iteration (Stages 8–9)

**C-50 · Every upload is evidence.** Write down the bets before publishing and score them afterwards.
*Check: what did we bet, and what happened?*

**C-51 · Diagnose the funnel after every upload.** Follow it from views to leads to qualified calls to customers to revenue. Find the stage where it dropped off (traffic, CTA, funnel or sales) and fix that stage. This needs tracking for each video.
*Check: which stage did worse than for this video's peers?*

**C-52 · Double down on the reason a video worked, not on the video itself.** Keep that reason and find a genuinely new or better way to use the same signal.
*Check: what was the signal, and what's the new angle?*

**C-53 · Research done before publishing is a hypothesis.** Nothing is guaranteed, and the market gets the final vote.
*Check: did we let the result update the system?*

**C-54 · Decisions need execution.** Poor editing and inconsistent output can ruin good decisions. Every week, a competent editor carries the decisions out.
*Check: does the editor have a brief that says what to remove and what not to add?*

## Engine defaults

These numbers are the engine's starting assumptions, not Trinder's. Tune them as evidence comes in, and record every change in the changelog.

| Setting | Default | Where it's set |
|---|---|---|
| Outlier threshold | ≥ 3× the channel's baseline | `research/config.json` |
| Minimum views to count as an outlier | 10,000 | `research/config.json` |
| Long-form cutoff | ≥ 181 seconds, since Shorts can run to 3 minutes | `research/config.json` |
| "Repeated evidence" for a topic or format | ≥ 3 outliers across ≥ 2 channels, with at least one from the last 12 months | Stage 2 gate |
| Baseline for our own videos | Median views of the previous 10 videos at the same checkpoint (needs 3+) | `yt.py diagnose` |
| Bottleneck flag | A funnel stage below 0.8× its usual rate | `yt.py diagnose` |
